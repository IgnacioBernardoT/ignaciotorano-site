The Stovall House on Bayshore Boulevard

One of Bayshore Boulevard’s best-documented surviving grand estates and the longtime home of Tampa Tribune publisher Wallace F. Stovall.

At 4621 Bayshore Boulevard, the Stovall House survives from an era when much of Tampa’s waterfront boulevard was defined by large private estates rather than towers. The City of Tampa’s preservation record identifies it as a 1909 Neoclassical residence built for Logan T. and Florence M. Trousdale.

In 1915, the Trousdales sold the property to Wallace F. Stovall, founder and longtime publisher of The Tampa Tribune. Tampa’s landmark record says Stovall lived here for about 30 years. That long association is why the estate carries his name today.

Who Was Wallace F. Stovall?

Stovall was a major figure in Tampa publishing and civic life. His connection to The Tampa Tribune placed him among the city’s influential business leaders during decades when downtown, South Tampa and the waterfront were changing quickly. City preservation staff consider the house one of the strongest surviving physical links to his life.

That link is especially important because many of the residences once associated with Tampa’s early business elite along Bayshore have disappeared. The City’s landmark materials explicitly note that only a small number of those grand homes survive.

Francis J. Kennard and the Architecture

City preservation records attribute the design to Tampa architect Francis J. Kennard. The home’s Neoclassical character is expressed through its formal symmetry, gabled portico and large classical columns. Those features were designed to read as a grand estate from Bayshore Boulevard and from the extensive grounds around the house.

The National Park Service added the Stovall House to the National Register of Historic Places in 1974. Tampa later designated it a local historic landmark in 2022 after a public preservation review.

From Private Estate to Adaptive Reuse

During the 2022 landmark hearing, City staff described the property as originally a single-family home that had been adapted for use as a private club. The same hearing emphasized how unusual it is for an intact early-20th-century estate house to remain on Bayshore.

The property is therefore useful for understanding the history of Bayshore itself. The boulevard has remained premium real estate for generations, but the form of that luxury has changed. Large private lawns and detached estates gradually gave way to denser residential development and condominium towers.

Did You Know?

The City’s 2022 landmark presentation noted that the Stovall property once extended much farther west than it does today, illustrating how the original estate land was subdivided as South Tampa developed.
